# FACE DOWN

9:16 · 38 seconds · phone-first · Concept and brief by the agent, 2026-11-18

An open-invitation brief. The director asked the agent to take the mic: develop a

## Point of view

The film is shot from inside a phone. The frame is the screen, looking out. We see
only while the screen is lit: a face, from below, for the few seconds it takes
someone to type one line. Then the screen sleeps and we are in the dark until the
next line.

That is the literal point of view. The one the film argues is this:

**What reaches the screen is a face looking down and one line. The day is what
happens in the dark between the lines, and the dark is the better part.**

The recent phone shorts in this repository (HOLD, REMOVE, UNMUTE, REPEAT, POLISH)
all put the interface at the centre and let it take something from a person. This
one stands on the other side of the glass and says the opposite. The screen is the
one left out. Nobody is harmed by that. It is the right arrangement, and the film
is glad of it.

So there is no villain, no notification, no prompt, no assistant character. The
phone never asks for anything. It gets less and less, and the film treats that as
the good news.

## The film

One person, one day, five lines. Each time she picks the phone up we get her face
and she types a short report. Each time the screen sleeps we get black and the
sound of her day, heard from wherever the phone is.

Two things change as the day goes on, and they are the whole structure:

- **The lines get shorter.** Seven words, then two, then three, then one, then a
  sentence she does not finish.
- **The dark gets longer and clearer.** Two seconds through a pocket, then longer,
  then longer again, and the sound opens up as the phone moves from pocket to bag
  to countertop to nightstand. By the end the dark is seven seconds of a room we
  can hear perfectly and cannot see.

The last dark is different in kind. Every earlier black is the screen going to
sleep. The last one is her putting the phone face down, still lit, mid-sentence,
because someone has spoken to her.

## Rules of the build

1. **We see only while the screen is lit.** No establishing shots, no cutaways, no
   second angle. If it is not a face looking down at a phone, it is black.
2. **Black is true black.** Not dark grey, no vignette, no grain, no waveform, no
   caption. On a phone held in the hand, a true-black frame turns the glass into a
   mirror, and the viewer sees their own face looking down at the same angle she
   has. That reflection is part of the picture and the reason this is a phone film.
3. **The dark is where the events are.** The run, the fix at work, the drive home
   and dinner all happen in the black. The faces show only what those things left
   on her.
4. **The lines arrive intact and plain.** Each line types on in the lower third in
   a neutral system sans, white, one character per key tick, and reads the right
   way round. On send it lifts up and off the top of the frame. A mirrored
   rendering would be more literal and was rejected: the line is the one thing
   that gets through whole, so it has to be legible at a glance.
5. **She never speaks on camera.** Her mouth stays closed in every lit shot except
   the laugh in S4 and the smile in S5. Other people are heard and never seen.
   No intelligible sentence of dialogue anywhere in the film; the only words are
   the five lines and one quiet "yes" in the dark.
6. **No score.** The day is the soundtrack.
7. **No title card.** The film ends on the black. The title lives in the
   description.

## Picture direction

- **Camera:** the phone's own position. Held at chest height, tilted up, a wide
  lens close to the face, the small drift and correction of a hand. Chin, nostrils,
  the underside of the jaw: the unflattering, honest angle everyone knows from
  catching themselves in a dark screen. Do not prettify it.
- **Light:** two sources in every shot. The place (window grey, noon sky, office
  overhead, kitchen tungsten, bedside lamp) and a faint cool lift from below,
  which is us. The cool lift is strongest in S1 and S5 where the room is dim.
- **Her:** one woman in her late forties, short dark curly hair going grey, no
  styling to speak of. Clothes change with the day: a sleep shirt, a running top,
  a collared shirt, the same shirt with the sleeves pushed up, a sleep shirt. One
  character reference carried across all five shots.
- **Her eyes:** on us in S1. A little less in every shot after. In S4 a glance. In
  S5 she starts on us and leaves. Attention moving away from the screen across the
  day is the performance; it should never read as sadness or rejection. She is
  busy living.
- **Wake and sleep:** wake is a hard cut in with three frames of brightness ramp.
  Sleep is a hard cut to black on the click, usually while the phone is already
  moving toward a pocket, so the last lit frames are a swing of ceiling or cloth.
- **The turn in S5:** the only camera move that matters. The phone rotates away
  from her, the lamp streaks through, the wood grain rises to meet the lens and
  the frame goes black by contact. This can be a generated move or a local
  construction; it must not cut.
- **No legible text in the world.** No readable monitor, no signage, no clock.

## Sound direction

The sound carries more than half the running time and all of the plot.

- **Method (revised 2026-11-18 after the director's question):** generated sound
  effects are the default for the darks. The rule that matters is how they are
  asked for: **each dark is one continuous scene, generated as a single take from
  one description of a place and what is happening in it**, not assembled from a
  list of separate cues. A zip, then laces, then a door, each placed by hand,
  reads as a checklist of signifiers; one take of someone leaving the house with
  a phone in their pocket has the overlaps and accidents a real minute has. Keep
  what a take volunteers. Layer by hand only to join two places inside one dark
  (D2, D3).
- **Test D4 and D5 first.** They are two people being easy with each other, and
  the ending rests on them. If generated effects cannot give unscripted-sounding
  voices in a room, generate those two darks as video with native audio and
  discard the picture. That is the only place the original idea (stage every
  dark as footage and throw the footage away) is worth its cost.
- **Where the phone is sets how it hears.** D1 pocket: heavy low-pass, cloth
  rustle in time with the steps. D2 pocket then bag: slightly more open. D3 desk:
  clear and near, then pocket again for the trip home. D4 countertop: fully open,
  a little hard-surface reflection. D5 face down on wood: open, warm, close. The
  filter is built locally so the opening-up is deliberate.
- **Screen-off click** before D1 to D4. None before D5.
- **Key ticks** are quiet and dry, one per character, and their rhythm tells us
  how much attention each line got: steady in S1, quick in S2, clipped in S3,
  three careless taps in S4, slow in S5 and then nothing.
- **Voices** other than hers are texture. If a model returns a legible sentence,
  keep it only if it is ordinary; do not caption it.
- Level the darks so the film is not quiet. D5 should be the most present sound in
  the piece, not the faintest.

## Known risks

- **A model will try to make her talk.** Generated people facing a camera tend to
  speak, with or without audio. Check her mouth on dense frames in every lit shot
  before accepting a take, against the one thing the premise rests on: she types,
  she does not speak.
- **Black is swipe-away territory.** The film opens on a face, not on black, the
  first dark is only two seconds and is loud with running, and every dark starts
  with a recognisable sound in its first half second. Do not lengthen D1.
- **The low angle can drift toward a flattering selfie.** It should look like a
  screen's view of a person, not a person's view of herself.
- **Character drift across five lighting setups.** One reference, checked shot to
  shot at the hairline and jaw, which is what this angle shows.
- **The mirror only works on true black.** Check the encoded file for lifted
  blacks after every export, and review on an actual phone in the hand.

## Open to the director

- The five lines. They are written to shrink and to say less than the dark does;
  the wording is replaceable.
- Whether she is this woman. Any one person works. The film should not look like
  a specific real person.
- A title card, if the black ending reads as a broken upload instead of an ending.

## Agent's additions

Everything above the format line. In particular: the inside-the-phone frame, the
shrinking lines against the lengthening dark, the true-black mirror, the rule that
each dark is one continuous take, the face-down ending with the unsent
line, and the title.

## Draft v1 (2026-11-18)

Built the day the brief was written, on the director's go-ahead. 39.9 seconds, 1080 by 1920. Current state and
the open decisions are on the status page ([status.json](status.json)).

- **Picture.** One character reference, five from-below anchors, five Veo 3.1 Quality takes at the first attempt.
  Each lit shot is one unbroken range of one take. Four of the five takes drew thumbs or typing fingers at the
  bottom edge, which the cut keeps as the typing. The takes end on their own swing away from her face where the
  swing came clean (morning, desk) and on her looking away where it did not (run, kitchen).
- **The turn.** The bedside take looked up, smiled and began to turn, then showed the room instead of going face
  down. The last third of a second is built locally from the take's own swing, a wood plate and black.
- **The line.** Left-aligned just below the middle of the frame, above the Shorts interface zone, instead of the
  lower third. One character per tick; the first line breaks after "badly." so nothing reflows.
- **Sound.** The first three darks are generated effects, one take per place, asked for clean and muffled locally
  by where the phone is. For the last two darks the brief's by-ear test could not be run by an agent that cannot
  listen, so both are the sound of video takes with the picture discarded, and a generated-effects alternate is
  kept. Those takes wrote ordinary dialogue. Under the bedside shot a man says "Hey there."; after the phone goes
  down he asks "Long day, huh?" and she answers "Yeah." in the dark. The unsent line is "Today was". That was not
  written; it is kept, uncaptioned, pending the director's ruling on rule 5.
- **Missing from the darks.** The dog, the tap, the stairs and a settled breath: the takes did not volunteer them
  and nothing was layered in to fake them.
- **Agent's additions in the build.** The split of the bedroom take across the turn, so the greeting is heard in
  the light and the answer in the dark; the line dimming out one second before the end, as a screen finally
  sleeping.

## To publication: v2 to v4 and the 4K (2026-11-19 to 2026-11-20)

The director's notes after watching v1, each a local re-render with no new generation:

- **The last dark ends on "Yeah."** The take went on to a laugh and "Come here."; both are cut. The fade to black
  stays. The unsent line holds its cursor, dims and leaves twelve frames of black.
- **The desk line reads "Not finishing by noon."** It is typed faster so that it still sends before the phone goes
  down.
- **No spoken word in the long dark.** The take's "Yes." read as too breathy. A replacement in a stock voice was
  tried and the director turned it down ("the voice isn't quite right"), so the word is gone and the dark is trimmed:
  typing runs straight into the car. The film is 36.5 seconds.
- **4K finish of v4.** The lit shots are the textless takes upscaled two times and blended half-and-half with a plain
  resize, because the upscaling model alone etched skin and hair on faces this close (the agent's call). The typed
  lines and the darks are drawn natively at the finishing size. The soundtrack is v4's, unchanged.
- **Left as built, without a ruling:** the line position, the video-take sound for the last two darks, the locally
  built turn, and no title card.

The agent cannot listen; every statement about sound here is a measurement or a transcription.
