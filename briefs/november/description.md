NOVEMBER (2026).

A small harbor town puts the last ship to bed for the winter: the haul up the rollers, the stones packed round the hull, the plug pulled so the rain drains instead of rotting the planks. The sailor carries the steering oar home and hangs it over the fire. Then the winter. No music; nobody speaks.

The Romans closed the sea when the Pleiades set. Vegetius, Epitoma rei militaris 4.39: "A Novembri autem mense crebris tempestatibus navigia conturbat Vergiliarum hiemalis occasus … Ex die igitur tertio idus Novembres usque in diem sextum idus Martias maria clauduntur." From November the winter setting of the Pleiades troubles ships with storm after storm; so from the third day before the Ides of November to the sixth before the Ides of March, the seas are closed.

Pliny, Natural History 2.125: "Vergiliarum occasus hiemem inchoat, quod tempus in III idus Novembres incidere consuevit." The setting of the Pleiades begins winter, a date that usually falls on the eleventh of November.

The closed sea was law as well as custom. An edict of 380 to the African shipowners who carried the state grain, Codex Theodosianus 13.9.3: "Novembri mense navigatione subtracta …" With navigation withdrawn in the month of November.

What the crew does to the ship is older than Rome. Hesiod, Works and Days 618–632 (Evelyn-White): "when the Pleiades plunge into the misty sea … then truly gales of all kinds rage … Haul up your ship upon the land and pack it closely with stones all round … draw out the bilge-plug so that the rain of heaven may not rot it … Put away all the tackle and fittings in your house, and stow the wings of the sea-going ship neatly … hang up the well-shaped rudder over the smoke."

November, from novem, nine: the ninth month of the old Roman year. The bronze figure on the shelf is Isis-Fortuna, who holds a ship's rudder; her festival of the ship reopened the sea in March.
