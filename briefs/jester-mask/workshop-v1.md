# Jester Mask — horror workshop

Status: creative development. The treatments below are assistant proposals, not
a selected direction or a record of completed production.

## Director's starting point

A 9:16 horror short, 10–15 seconds, built around the supplied jester mask.
Heightened scary-movie visuals drive the piece. Every second must create
anticipation, change what we understand, or deliver the scare. The initial
image is a mask in pure black with eyes appearing inside it. Sound should imply
a larger situation without spending screen time on exposition.

## What the reference gives us

The reference shows a glossy white mask with asymmetric black decoration,
long black tear shapes, red jewels and red lips. Its fixed expression has a
mournful quality. The hollow sockets are the natural point of attention.
Hard reflections on the lacquer can make the surface feel expensive, cold
and touchable. Black decoration can disappear into a completely black surround,
leaving an incomplete white face suspended in space.

The preserved image is a square, angled close-up with part of the perimeter
cropped. A complete floating mask in a portrait composition would require a
new anchor or reconstruction; the supplied image alone does not show those
missing edges. Preserve its particular paint, jewels and proportions through
that work. Original creation model and prompt are unknown.

## First recommendation: ENCORE

An unseen theatre is forcing something to perform. Human eyes appear inside
the mask, visibly frightened. When the mask falls, the eyes remain in the dark.
The audience is still there, and it is much closer than we thought.

Working duration: **13 seconds**, with a proposed 24 fps / 312-frame edit.
The timing is an editorial proposal, not a provider request.

| Time | Picture | Sound and purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0–1 s | Black for only a few frames, then a hard sliver of light finds the white cheek and red lip. | One distant theatre cue bell. It establishes a performance before we see a location. |
| 1–3 s | The mask becomes legible, hanging at a slight angle in absolute black. Its sockets are empty. | A small, close breath seems to come from inside the hard shell. The last bell resonance dies. |
| 3–6 s | Two tiny wet catchlights approach from deep inside the sockets. Real human eyes arrive behind the openings, wide and frightened. Their emergence has depth rather than a simple opacity fade. | A restrained rising string friction, with a tiny moist eyelid sound as the eyes settle. |
| 6–8 s | The eyes glance just past the camera, then freeze. The mask remains rigid. | A calm, distant voice: “Again.” The breath catches; the tonal bed cuts away. |
| 8–10 s | A retaining click; the mask drops sharply out of the bottom of the portrait frame. The eyes stay at precisely the same height. There is no visible face around them. | A dry fastening click, a quick scrape, then a small hard impact below frame. The separation of shell and eyes must read cleanly. |
| 10–11.5 s | Hold the impossible eyes long enough to understand them. One involuntary blink. Cut to black. | A short, scraped string accent at the reveal, followed by exposed breathing. |
| 11.5–13 s | Black. | One solitary clap, now right beside us. End on its short tail. The audience has crossed the distance. |

The emotional change is curiosity, recognition of distress, then an impossible
release. The eyes need believable lids, moisture, small movements and shadow.
Their fear supplies the humanity. The mask's beautiful fixed expression supplies
the cruelty. The final held eyes are the principal visual payoff; avoid adding
several competing transformations.

A harder scare ending is an alternative to the final held blink: after the
mask falls, the eyes make one abrupt move toward the lens, then black. Audition
this as a different ending if the stillness does not carry enough menace.

## Other directions worth exploring

- **YOUR TURN — possession.** A quiet voice says “Hold still.” The eyes inside
  the suspended mask seem to measure us. It snaps toward the lens; a fastening
  clicks shut in darkness. Our next breath has the close, hollow resonance of
  someone breathing inside it. The final image could be the world seen through
  its two eye openings. The implied story is a fitting that cannot be refused.
- **RED TEARS — the object becomes flesh.** One red jewel loosens, becomes a
  bead of wet red, and crawls upward into an empty socket. A human eye opens
  exactly where it disappears. The black painted tear moves a fraction after
  everything else has stopped. An antique display-case latch and glass ticks
  suggest something that should have stayed sealed. This is a tactile,
  surreal body-horror branch.
- **DON'T BLINK — stalking through discontinuity.** Each brief blackout leaves
  the mask substantially closer, with no visible travel. Sound stays continuous
  through the cuts. At the last distance we discover its eyes looking past us
  in terror; a floorboard creaks immediately behind the listener. The apparent
  pursuer has been watching something else approach.

## Visual and sonic vocabulary

Keep a severe white / black / jewel-red palette. Use a hard side light and
deeply recessed sockets, saving an intimate view of wet eyes for the turn.
The vertical frame makes a falling mask especially clear: the empty space
below it becomes part of the action. Keep the relevant eyes large enough to
read on a phone. The reference's glamour should survive the horror.

Useful free associations: an understudy who never leaves; applause that is a
command; a display label glimpsed for less than a second; a theatre cue bell
answered by a breath; a porcelain tap answered from the inside; jewels that
reflect an audience we never see; an eye that is already crying when it arrives;
a smile held long after the performer wants to stop. These are ingredients,
not instructions to put every idea in the film.

Build tension through sound distance and texture: a cue far away, breath inside
the object, and a clap suddenly close. Keep the crucial events legible in mono
and on a phone speaker. Reserve the sharp transient for the visual rupture;
let silence make room for it. Avoid a continuous wall of score. A title card
would consume useful time, so the proposed cut carries no opening title.
