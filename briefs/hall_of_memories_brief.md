Status: factory cold-open direction and rounded dark-grey robot look approved;

## Clarified story direction

The relationship between the Hall and humanity's disappearance remains
ambiguous. The user has clarified that some fraction of humanity became
transhuman and continued as the inhabitants or predecessors of the polis.
The rest faded away by choice. Imply this history through images and/or a few
lines rather than explaining a definitive extinction event.

The Hall emphasizes the painful parts of human experience: pain, sadness,
betrayal and related suffering. Its screams express the weight of that
experience. A specific person's record and voice may make the demonstration
concrete; that approach remains a creative option, not a locked sequence.

The exact nature of the remaining humans' choice is still open. Gradually
choosing not to have children is a proposed implication below, not an additional
user-established fact. The last human's identity, death and date remain unknown.

## Format and production intent

- One continuous finished film, landscape 16:9, with the hard cuts, transitions
  and time lapses described below. Production chapters need not be visible acts
  or separate episodes in the finished film.
- Intended as the longest continuous stop-motion film in this project so far.
  Determine duration through further planning, performance and pacing tests.
- Prefer stop motion and local animation, extending the layered artwork,
  discrete poses and articulated props of Scary Woods and the persistent sets
  and repeatable camera paths of Meridian House.
- User clarification after the first board: at minimum, the factory framing
  story and The Exchange must be photorealistic, in different registers. The
  factory should look like a stereotypically depressed workplace; The Exchange
  should be more cinematic. The board's geometric people and sets are blocking
  placeholders, not the finish target for these passages. Other passages' final
  visual treatment remains open. Audition motion cadence with the chosen imagery.
- Further user clarification, 2026-09-24: in the factory, the man, setting and
  equipment remain photorealistic, while the robots are deliberately more unreal
  and visually match the polis inhabitants. A noticeable realism contrast is
  required. The first mechanical, photorealistic robot proposal is superseded.
- Subsequent robot refinement: darker grey, smoother and more rounded forms,
  with unnaturally luminous eyes that are brighter yellow and more orange.
  Keep the factory robots and polis inhabitants in the same visual family.
  The user approved the selected rounded wide and frontal close-up references
  on 2026-09-24. This approves their visual direction and coverage principle,
  not a complete shot list or motion performance.
- Generating the entire film with Veo is cost prohibitive. Develop a selective
  hybrid approach around local animation; photorealism does not itself authorize
  full-scene Veo generation or establish a paid chapter ceiling.
- Establish a budget for each chapter as work proceeds. Assume a minimum $20
  floor per chapter unless changed by the user. This is a planning floor,
  not a spending target, a fixed ceiling or an approved total for this outline.
- Rough local blocking and several explicitly requested look-frame auditions
  exist; see the audition notes. Designs beyond the approved factory robot
  direction remain open. That still-generation
  authorization does not establish a paid ceiling for chapter production.

## Premise and dramatic sequence

### 1. The last human on the assembly line

Selected cold-open direction: a darkly comic factory legend that initially
plays as the film's reality. Its growing contradictions reveal that a polis
child is inventing the account while other children question it. Every attempted
repair changes the scene until it becomes impossible to continue.

The underlying task is an execution assembly line operated by a bored human
worker under robot supervision. Executions remain implied offscreen: queued
people, an obscured processing chamber, routine machine sounds and the eventual
dialogue establish what is happening. No blade contact, severed anatomy or gore
is shown. The comedy comes from workplace routine, institutional rules and the
children's collapsing explanation.

#### Robot coverage and spatial continuity

User clarification for the final edit, 2026-09-24: robots do not appear at the
beginning of this chapter. They appear only sparsely until the last two thirds,
with coverage ramping toward the end. Preserve this qualitative progression;
exact reveal times are not locked. The first work-routine production excerpt
contains no robots. A separate robot performance test is not its opening shot.

User blocking direction: except for roughly one or two establishing shots,
mostly show the robots in straight-on close-up, framed from the neck or shoulders
upward. Use editing to clarify their position relative to the man, machinery and
other subjects. This governs robot coverage, not every shot in the factory.
Retain worker actions, prop inserts and the scripted visual contradictions.
Robot performance should remain legible through restrained head/posture changes,
timing and the relationship between cuts; mouths are not required.

#### Opening: an ordinary workday

Begin without narration or any indication that this is a story being told.
A tired man clocks in, adjusts his stool and operates a lever. An industrial
clunk. He marks a clipboard. Another clunk. He checks the clock. Allow enough
patience for the audience to accept the scene as the actual world of the film.

Gradually widen the composition to reveal people being carried toward the
obscured chamber. Keep robots out of the opening; introduce them later according
to the coverage progression above. Two grey, yellow-eyed robots can eventually
be established behind the worker, visibly pleased with his work. One may present
an employee-of-the-month plaque; he
barely acknowledges it. Their happiness should read through posture and gesture
within the featureless humanoid design.

#### The first interruption

Narration arrives as the robots' satisfaction becomes difficult to reconcile
with the work. Dialogue below is the accepted working version, still subject
to performance and timing refinement:

> Storyteller: They kept one human to finish the work.
>
> Another child: Why didn't they do it themselves?
>
> Storyteller: Robots weren't allowed to kill people.

One robot proudly indicates a workplace safety notice.

> Another child: But they made him do it.
>
> Storyteller: That was allowed.

Hold briefly on the robots' completely innocent satisfaction.

#### The last-human contradiction

> Child: Who killed the last human?
>
> Storyteller: The next shift.

A second, identical worker enters and reaches for the clipboard.

> Child: You said there was only one.

The second worker backs through the door. The door disappears. The first
worker notices. The storytelling now visibly rewrites the physical set.

> Storyteller: Fine. There was a timer.

A timer abruptly appears on the machine.

> Child: Then why did they need the man?

This is the central contradiction: the proposed repair makes the original
human worker unnecessary.

> Storyteller: The timer wasn't allowed to kill people either.
>
> Child: It's a timer.
>
> Storyteller: It was different then.

Two yellow eyes illuminate on the timer.

#### Collapse and hard cut

Corrections make the workplace increasingly incoherent. Controls relocate.
Another "last human" keeps appearing behind the wrong door. The robots point
at the man, the man points at the timer, and the timer displays:

> HUMAN AUTHORIZATION REQUIRED

Nobody can finish the story. The children's voices overlap. The worker looks
directly toward their source. Hard cut to the polis circle, with the storyteller
continuing the same emphatic gesture just made by the worker:

> Storyteller: Will you let me finish?

The visual match reveals the factory's people as puppets of the child's
imagination. The elder can enter during the ensuing argument. Each visual
contradiction should follow a specific spoken objection so the escalation reads
as storytelling rather than random visual errors.

#### The tellers and the elder

Current production focus, 2026-09-26: the user accepted the
[children's conversation](hall_of_memories_polis_scene.md) as a useful baseline,
with targeted future revisions intended to affect the polis portions together.
The user requested a first draft of [the elder's invitation](hall_of_memories_invitation.md)
through a natural stopping point. It ends at the Hall threshold before the first
record. On 2026-09-27 the user accepted the continuation with no notes, directing
that the polis scenes be reworked as a whole after the core is complete. Keep
building forward from these baselines; see the latest next-session handoff for
the recommended Hall-demonstration boundary. The factory motion test is
preserved and not implicitly approved by this switch.

The circle contains juvenile grey humanoid robots with glowing yellow eyes,
telling legends about how and why humanity vanished. One child asks the exact
line, placed after the cold-open argument as the discussion turns serious:

> Where is everybody?

The sky is strange. Colors are muted and different from the introductory scene.
These humanoids are software inhabitants sharing physical computing hardware;
their visible bodies and surroundings exist inside a simulation. The environment
looks strange because it is unreal. Specific visual rules remain for auditions.
They continue the branch of humanity that became transhuman. The children may
have originated within the polis; do not assume every resident once personally
had a biological body.

An older humanoid overhears. He tells them that no one knows when the last human
died, but he can show them why. He leads them into a large building named the
Hall of Memories. Age here describes presentation and social role; the mechanics
of software childhood and aging have not been defined.

The factory offers the children's proposed explanation that machines caused
humanity's disappearance. The Hall introduces the possibility that human
experience and human choices shaped that history. Preserve the ambiguity of
the elder's interpretation and give the first individual record room to change
the tone from gallows humor to recognizable suffering.

The Human Recall and The Last Evacuation remain unselected fallback concepts:
the first is a defective-product recall whose last human clerk cannot process
their own return; the second is an evacuation whose destination, pilot and
capacity keep changing until Earth attempts to depart from another Earth.
Develop the selected factory opening unless the user changes direction.

### 2. The Hall of Memories

The Hall contains extraordinarily granular lifetime records of billions of
people: every digital record, biometrics, DNA and related information.

The elder plays the sound one person would make if they could fully understand
their life: a scream. The original outline described weighing the good and bad;
the clarified emphasis is the weight of pain, sadness, betrayal and other
suffering across a life. The presentation should communicate lived experience.
He adds more people. Individual voices accumulate into a cacophony, then into
barely recognizable noise.

The camera withdraws from the demonstration and the building while the sound
grows. It continues beyond the simulated world to reveal the physical polis
from outside: a large jewel.

The elder offers a perspective on why humanity faded away. The film leaves room
between his interpretation, the records and the audience's conclusion. The
literal machinery producing the whole-life sound remains unspecified. An
archive record is not automatically a living reconstruction; conscious
reenactment or torture is not established by this premise.

### 3. The jewel and deep time

The pullback reveals the polis deep underground. A cutaway of Earth makes its
position in relation to crust, mantle and surface readable. Geological time has
carried it downward; subsequent time lapses show it moving up and down with the
changing Earth.

Ice ages come and go. After the latest ice age, plants return and jungles form.
No large animals are visible. The landscape should have time to register as a
living but strangely uninhabited world.

A spacecraft lands. Aliens briefly explore the surface. One speaks to another
in an alien language. The subtitle repeats the child's exact question:

> Where is everybody?

The spacecraft leaves. The aliens do not discover the buried polis in this
outline. Their visit is a pause in the geological movement, long enough for the
question and the apparent emptiness to register.

### 4. Humanity returns

Another time lapse shows complex animal life developing again, eventually
producing humans again. Simultaneously, geological change deposits the jewel
on the surface. A rough man's hand picks it up.

Human recurrence is part of this speculative premise. Its biological mechanism
and the timescale remain open; do not invent a surviving human enclave, a polis
terraforming program or alien intervention to explain it.

### 5. The feast and the exchange

Transition to a cave or primitive-looking hall. Victorious warriors feast
rowdily. One boldly addresses the elder or chief. With no subtitles, gestures
make clear that he demands the chief's adult daughter in marriage as a war prize.
Both the chief and his daughter are unhappy about it.

The warrior produces the same jewel retrieved from the surface. She is entranced.
Her fascination with it is a separate beat from her reaction to the demand.
Do not silently turn fascination into an expression of consent to the exchange.

### 6. The daughter and the witnesses

Later that night, the feast has become significantly more rowdy. The adult
daughter is on a bed of furs, gazing into the jewel, which she has strung into a
necklace. The surrounding context implies the consummation of the imposed union;
sexual activity is not shown.

Hard cut inside the polis. A large crowd of featureless grey humanoids with
yellow eyes has assembled around the Hall of Memories. They are looking up,
watching her look into the jewel. Nobody moves. Their stillness and composition
convey deep concern.

The means by which the polis observes the outside is not yet designed. Preserve
the shared gaze without assuming a particular screen, sky projection or sensor.

### 7. The closing sound transformation

The user has proposed two staging alternatives:

1. Return to the daughter. Fade to black as the feast grows louder. Hold black
   while the party sound becomes a baby's cry.
2. Stay with the unmoving crowd around the Hall. Let the party become a baby's
   cry while they watch, then fade to black without showing the outside again.

The baby is the child of the daughter and warrior; the sound transition implies
roughly nine months passing. Hold on black for a couple of seconds as the baby's
cry becomes the same screaming noise heard earlier in the Hall. Crescendo, then
exactly 0.25 seconds of silence. The placement of any credits remains undecided;
do not insert a logo, musical resolution or other sound into that final silence.

## Directorial recommendations — provisional

### Implying the human inheritance

Preferred approach: combine a very short exchange with a visual relationship
between a human archive image and a grey avatar. Keep human-to-polis continuity
legible without an upload-machine explanation.

Possible dialogue, for discussion rather than recording:

> Child: Where is everybody?
>
> Elder: Some of us are still here.

Let the child study him. At the Hall entrance, a human portrait and an avatar
could share the same pose or small habitual gesture. A matched movement gives
the audience a connection to discover. This could be an archival transition
showing a founder; identifying that human specifically as the elder is a
stronger autobiographical variant that has not been chosen.

The user-specified exchange about the last human still belongs in the scene:
the elder does not know when the last human died, but says he can show why.
Exact wording and the placement of the child's repeated question can be refined
when scripting the circle and walk to the Hall.

To keep the nature of the choice implicit, the preferred second exchange to
audition is:

> Child: And the others?
>
> Elder: Not everyone wanted forever.

This suggests a choice about continuing without specifying the course of the
last biological generations. If we want a clearer implication of declining
births instead, an alternative is:

> Child: And the others?
>
> Elder: Fewer children, every year. In the end ... none.

That conveys the demographic shape without explaining every person's motives.
An individual record can supply evidence of choice. Keep the account partial;
the Hall's billions of records do not establish that every last person was
recorded or that the archive knows the last death. Complete detail about an
archived individual and incomplete knowledge of humanity's ending can coexist.

### One person before billions

Preferred Hall audition: one ordinary person's record, using their own recorded
voice and a small number of recurring images. Let us recognize a person before
hearing what a whole life becomes. The person need not be the elder or anyone
famous, exceptionally cruel or exceptionally unfortunate.

Start with a brief warm fragment: shared laughter or an affectionate ordinary
moment. Then allow pain, betrayal and bereavement to surface through sparse
fragments, with possible lines such as "It hurts," "You promised," and "I
thought we had more time." These are draft examples to replace with the
chosen person's specific circumstances, not a locked collection of catchphrases.
Avoid filling the Hall with a rapid montage of anonymous catastrophes.

If the declining-births interpretation is selected, one quiet fragment such as
"No. I don't want children" can establish this person's decision. Its placement
beside the rest of the record suggests a relationship to experience; the film
need not claim a universal motive for everyone who remained biological.

Only after the individual has registered should the elder call up the sound of
that person's complete life. The fragments are historical recordings; the
whole-life scream is the Hall's derived presentation, with its mechanism left
unexplained. Leave a beat after the first scream before introducing another
record and another voice. Then accelerate the accumulation into the withdrawal
from the building. No good/bad score, moral verdict or explanatory voice-over
is required for this proposed staging.

This route creates a connection to the closing crowd: they recognize the human
capacity to suffer in the woman's circumstances and in the coming child's
future. Their concern can carry compassion, remembered experience and fear of
recurrence without a line explaining which feeling dominates.

### Jewel and visual continuity

Choose an emerald: a saturated green object that stays recognizable against
muted simulated colors, rock, ice, foliage, skin and warm firelight. Its identity
should also be carried by one distinctive facet pattern or pale inclusion so
color alone does not have to connect the shots. Use the same master design from
the first exterior reveal to the necklace close-up. Exact size, finish and shape
need auditioning, including a credible way to secure a string around it.

Treat its appearance as an emerald-like housing for the fictional hardware.
Material durability, energy supply and observation mechanisms are worldbuilding
questions, not established properties of an ordinary gemstone.

Build the Hall and its forecourt once, then revisit their recognizable geometry
in the final crowd shot. The withdrawal from simulation to hardware can be a
conceptual transition aligned through a facet: it need not imply a literal tunnel
from the simulated building to the outside of the computer.

### Geological staging

Favor a tactile miniature cross-section with rock strata, folds, ice and surface
vegetation changing in stepped animation. Keep the jewel trackable as the crust
moves around it. Plan changes of scale explicitly; a human-sized pendant cannot
remain literally to scale in a planetary cutaway and still be readable.

Give the alien landing a comparatively patient, stable rhythm between time
lapses. Preserve the surface's emptiness during that visit. The later evolution
sequence needs distinct stages and elapsed-time cues so it reads as recurrence
across vast time rather than animals transforming overnight.

### The feast and implied union

Stage the demand in an intelligible sequence: warrior claims reward, indicates
the daughter, chief resists, daughter withdraws, jewel is revealed, her attention
fixes on it. Exact gestures and dialogue sounds require blocking; avoid assuming
that a busy wide shot will communicate the exchange.

For the later scene, keep her clothed on the furs, with the emerald taking her
attention. A partition separating the bed from the revelry and the warrior's
silhouette reaching the threshold can establish the situation. Cut to the polis
before any intimate action. Let the surrounding social power and sound carry
the implication without eroticizing her distress.

### Preferred ending

User decision, 2026-09-24: defer ending style decisions until beginning work on
the ending. The preference below remains an assistant proposal; neither ending
variant is newly approved, and deciding its style is not a prerequisite for
starting the factory.

Prefer the second ending: hold the concerned crowd through the change from feast
to infant cry, then fade to black. Their sustained attention links the private
human event to the archive's long memory. No robot needs to turn, blink, recoil
or explain what it means.

Keep the newborn cry identifiable before bringing back the Hall sound. Reuse
recognizable sonic material from the first demonstration so the connection is
audible. The interpretation to explore is recurrence of the human condition;
avoid assigning guilt to the newborn through an invented line or image.

Construct the crescendos through increasing voices, density and frequency
coverage as well as level. Retain separate source voices, crowd layers, party
ambience and infant sound so the transitions can be auditioned and adjusted.
At a proposed 24 fps, the final quarter-second is six frames; frame rate itself
has not yet been approved.

## Decisions for discussion

- Select how to imply the confirmed human-to-transhuman continuity. Is the elder
  personally a former biological human, or a later inhabitant carrying the
  community's history? How explicit should the dialogue be?
- The extinction relationship remains deliberately ambiguous. Decide whether
  to imply the remaining humans' choice through declining births, another
  approach, or no stated mechanism. The last death remains unknown.
- Audition an individual record and voice in the Hall. Choose the person,
  fragments and sound construction; preserve the painful-experience emphasis.
- Block and audition the selected factory cold open: establish the initial
  workplace rhythm, time the first voice-over, make each visual contradiction
  readable and land the matched-gesture cut. Determine its length through the
  animatic; keep the executions implied and the later Hall transition legible.
- Select simulated-world materials, sky, robot proportions, adult human design,
  alien forms and the jewel through later auditions.
- How is the vast archive made legible without a lengthy explanation? What does
  the audience understand about the distinction between records and inhabitants?
- How does outside perception appear to the polis crowd, and is the elder or
  an identifiable child recognizable among the final witnesses?
- Choose the final staging alternative, then establish runtime, chapter scopes
  and spending ceilings from a complete rough animatic.
